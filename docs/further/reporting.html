Reporting a survival analysis

What the methods, results, tables and figures of a prognostic study should contain, with a template built from the worked examples.

A survival analysis can only be judged if the reader knows how the times were defined and how much information lies behind each number. Reporting guidelines exist for observational studies in general (STROBE, Elm et al. (2007)), for prognostic marker studies (REMARK, McShane et al. (2005)) and for prediction models (TRIPOD, Collins et al. (2015)). The checklist below covers what they ask for in the analyses of this tutorial.

A template

The following paragraphs report the worked examples of this tutorial. Numbers come from the module; replace the software versions with yours (jamovi shows its own version under About jamovi, and the module’s version in the jamovi library of installed modules).

Statistical analysis. Overall survival was defined as the time from enrolment to death from any cause; patients alive at the last contact were censored. Survival was estimated with the Kaplan-Meier method, with 95% confidence intervals computed on the log scale, and follow-up was summarised with the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. Survival was compared between ECOG performance status groups with the log-rank test and the log-rank test for trend. Hazard ratios (HR) were estimated with Cox proportional hazards regression, univariable and adjusted for sex, age (per 10 years) and ECOG performance status (reference: ECOG 0); the proportional hazards assumption was assessed with scaled Schoenfeld residuals (Grambsch and Therneau 1994). Analyses used jamovi (version …) with the prognosis module (version 0.1.1), which uses the R package survival (Therneau 2026).

Results. Of 226 patients, 163 died during a median follow-up of 19.3 months. Median survival was 12.9, 10.1 and 6.5 months for ECOG 0, 1 and 2, and 1-year survival 54%, 43% and 21% (log-rank p < 0.001; trend p < 0.001). In the multivariable Cox model (Table 2), ECOG 1 and 2 were associated with higher mortality than ECOG 0 (HR 1.51, 95% CI 1.02–2.23, and 2.47, 95% CI 1.58–3.86), and women had lower mortality than men (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.42–0.81). The crude association of age (HR per 10 years 1.20, 95% CI 1.00–1.44) weakened after adjustment (HR 1.11, 95% CI 0.93–1.34). There was no evidence against proportional hazards (global test p = 0.288).

Common mistakes

  • No number at risk under a Kaplan-Meier plot, or curves drawn to the last patient, where the steps are huge and the estimates meaningless.
  • “Median survival not reached” reported as a number, or survival at a time beyond the follow-up.
  • Only p-values, no hazard ratios or survival probabilities with CIs.
  • Univariable screening (“variables with p < 0.05 entered the multivariable model”) without saying so; it biases the results and should be justified.
  • Too many variables for the number of events.
  • Subgroup claims without an interaction test (see the subgroup fallacy).
  • Hazard ratios described as risks: “a 42% lower risk of death” for HR = 0.58 is common but loose; “a 42% lower hazard (rate) of death” is correct.
  • Censoring the wrong patients: for overall survival, deaths from any cause are events, not censored observations.
